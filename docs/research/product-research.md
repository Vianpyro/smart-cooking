# SmartCooking: Research to Inform a Product Vision (PRD)

## TL;DR
- **The recipe-website category is saturated and structurally contracting, and a solo, no-budget, community-UGC entrant has poor odds of winning on "community" — but a genuine, defensible niche exists: a fast, ad-free, cook-focused tool for beginners on a budget, seeded as curated content first and community-second.** SmartCooking should be positioned as a *tool + curated starter library*, not a *community platform*, and should treat "community recipe sharing" as a deferred, opt-in layer rather than the core value proposition.
- **Of the three candidate differentiators, ingredient-based ("what can I make") search is real but already well-served (SuperCook) and hard to beat; per-serving cost estimation is genuinely wanted but effectively infeasible to automate accurately and honestly for an international audience; equipment-based filtering is the only candidate that is both under-served and achievable — but it is a minor feature, not a business.** Commit to equipment/skill-level filtering as a modest wedge; do NOT build automated cost estimation; treat pantry search as optional and secondary.
- **Organic search — the only realistic free acquisition channel — is collapsing for recipe content specifically (Google AI Overviews, AI "slop," short-form video), so a strategy that depends on ranking recipe pages in Google in 2026 is betting on a declining channel.** The realistic honest outcome is: this will cost money to run and will likely never make money; scope V1 accordingly, minimise running costs, and define success in usage/quality terms, not growth/revenue terms.

## Key Findings

### 1. The real problem is well-evidenced — but it is *decision* and *confidence*, not *lack of recipes*
- Beginner cooks and students do struggle, and the struggle is documented in peer-reviewed research. A Taylor & Francis pilot study of 338 undergraduates (*Journal of Hunger & Environmental Nutrition*, 2021) found 41.4% food-insecure and 21.0% with very low food security. A separate study of 338 public-university undergraduates published in the *Journal of Nutrition Education and Behavior* found food insecurity rose from 22.4% (2016) to 41.0% (2019), with "Almost half of the respondents (47.0%) reported experiencing food insecurity at least once." Low meal-preparation skill is repeatedly associated with worse diet quality and higher food insecurity, and cooking-facilities access and food-procurement skills measurably *reduce* food insecurity.
- The more useful behavioural insight is that the dominant pain is **decision fatigue** ("what do I make?"), not a shortage of recipes. The internet already has effectively infinite recipes; the friction is choosing, trusting, and executing. SuperCook's own reviewers and analysts note it "returns a long list instead of a decision."
- **Well-evidenced:** student food insecurity, low cooking skills, budget pressure, decision fatigue. **Anecdotal but strongly corroborated:** frustration with recipe-site UX (essays, ads, unreliable results).

### 2. What existing platforms do — and where they fail
- **The "long essay before the recipe" pattern is real, universally hated, and exists for concrete structural reasons: SEO (Google historically rewarded long dwell-time content) and copyright (a bare ingredient list is not protectable, so bloggers add original prose to have something to own and to monetise via ads/affiliates).** This is not irrational blogger vanity — it is a rational response to Google's and the ad industry's incentives. It is also the single clearest UX opening for a competitor.
- **Ad density and paywalls mid-cook** are the top complaints in 1–3 star reviews across the six most-installed recipe apps (Allrecipes, Yummly, NYT Cooking, Paprika, Mealime, Tasty), with full-screen and autoplay ads described as making apps "unusable mid-cook without Premium."
- **Business models vary:** Allrecipes/Tasty = ad-supported UGC libraries; NYT Cooking = curated subscription ($5–6/mo, a rare success — Nieman Lab documented it amassing 120,000 subscriptions in a year and a half after its 28 June 2017 paywall at $5/month, and it now anchors the NYT bundle); Paprika = one-time-purchase personal organiser ($4.99/platform); Cookpad = community UGC with a freemium tier (~$3/month).
- **Cookpad is the cautionary tale for community UGC.** Per Wikipedia/company reporting it has ~60 million monthly unique users in Japan and ~40 million globally (~100 million across 72 countries and 29 languages), relies on unpaid contributors, and has ~2 million premium subscribers — yet it has struggled to monetise (subscription revenue roughly flat/declining; three straight years of operating losses from 2021; headcount cut by more than half). It was "late to adapt to short smartphone video," and free video (YouTube/TikTok) drew users away. A community text-recipe archive is precisely the asset that short-form video and AI have devalued.
- **What has changed recently, and it is severe:** Food bloggers reported **30–80% traffic drops during 2025** attributed to Google AI Overviews/AI Mode. Ahrefs' April 2025 analysis of 300,000 keywords found that the presence of an AI Overview correlated with a 34.5% lower average click-through rate for the top-ranking page (Ahrefs revised this to a ~58% reduction in a December 2025 update). Seer Interactive's September 2025 study found organic CTR for AI-Overview queries fell from 1.76% to 0.61% (a ~61% decline), across 3,119 informational queries and 25.1M organic impressions. AI-generated "recipe slop" is flooding Google, Pinterest, and Facebook. Short-form video has displaced text recipes for younger users: Morning Consult (survey 30 Jan–4 Feb 2025, n=1,001 Gen Zers) found "Nearly half (45%) of Gen Zers said they were influenced by social media the last time they tried a new recipe," and a majority of Gen Z reportedly reach for TikTok/Reddit/YouTube before Google.

### 3. Which differentiators are actually valuable
- **(a) Equipment-based filtering — under-served, achievable, but minor.** Only meal-kit services (e.g., Hungryroot's "Cooking Appliances" filter) and appliance-brand blogs address "cook without an oven / microwave-only / dorm" use cases; no major recipe database makes equipment a first-class filter. For the target audience (students, dorm/shared-kitchen dwellers, first apartments) this maps directly to a real constraint. **Verdict: the only candidate differentiator that is both under-served and feasible — but it is a feature, not a moat.** Best framed together with skill level and time as "constraints-first" filtering.
- **(b) Ingredient/pantry search — real demand, already well-solved, hard to beat.** SuperCook is free, mature, pulls from millions of recipes, and is genuinely useful for "use up the fridge." Its documented failure modes: ingredient granularity mismatches (asks for green pepper when you have red; specific pasta types), it returns an overwhelming list rather than a decision, no portion scaling, no in-cook guidance, and data-loss complaints (lost pantries/favourites). A newcomer *could* do better on granularity and "decision, not list," but would be entering SuperCook's core turf with a fraction of the catalogue. **Verdict: do not lead with this; a cold-start catalogue makes pantry matching weak precisely because matching quality scales with catalogue size.**
- **(c) Per-serving cost estimation — wanted, but effectively infeasible to automate honestly.** This is the most important "no." There is **no free, global, current, ingredient-level grocery price API.** USDA ERS data is US-only and stale (the public Purchase-to-Plate National Average Prices file ends at 2017–18; the Fruit and Vegetable Prices dataset is produce-only, US-only, updated only every few years); UK ONS data is UK-only (though ONS is adding grocery scanner data for ~50% of the market in March 2026); Open Food Facts' "Open Prices" is crowdsourced, sparse, AGPL-licensed, and keyed to specific *barcoded products* rather than generic ingredients like "1 onion." Prices vary roughly 3–16x across countries (a Numbeo essentials basket ranged from ~$13.32 in Egypt to ~$39.67 in Australia) and food inflation swung from low-single-digits to ~20%+ annually in 2021–2023 (US food-at-home prices +11.4% in 2022; UK food inflation peaked at 19.1% in March 2023; eggs alone swung +32.2% in 2022 to a projected −30.7% in 2026), so a single number is both geographically meaningless for an international audience and stale within months. **Budget Bytes — the category leader for cost-per-serving — does it 100% manually: founder Beth Moncel states she calculates cost "by hand, for every single recipe on this website" using receipts and package labels, and staff have explicitly said it is "not feasible" to keep 1,500+ recipes updated.** Even Tandoor's "automated" cost calculation is just arithmetic over prices the user manually enters — no live price feed exists in any recipe app found. And users exhibit a well-documented ~40% "say-do gap" between stated grocery preferences and actual behaviour (though no study directly measures recipe-site cost labels). **Verdict: do not build automated cost estimation. At most, offer a coarse, honestly-hedged relative indicator ($/$$/$$$) or an optional user-entered cost — never a precise auto-calculated per-serving dollar figure.**

### 4. The cold-start problem is the binding constraint
- A community recipe site with no community is an empty room; the classic failure is spending the one-time launch traffic spike on an empty room. The literature is consistent: solve cold-start by (i) making the product useful to a *single* user with zero community (single-player utility — Andrew Chen's canonical framing), (ii) seeding content yourself under your real identity (legitimate) rather than faking multiple personas (fatal when discovered), (iii) starting with the smallest viable "atomic network" and a narrow, specific positioning rather than "everyone welcome."
- For a solo operator with no budget, the realistic path is **curated-content-first / tool-first**, treating UGC as a later opt-in layer. "Community recipe sharing" as the *core* positioning in 2026 is weak: it competes directly with the things (short-form video, AI answers) that have already hollowed out Cookpad and the food-blog web.

### 5. Table stakes vs differentiators
- **Table stakes (must exist to be taken seriously):** reliable search/filter; scalable servings; a distraction-free "cook mode" (large text, screen-stays-on, steps with inline quantities); save/favourite; print; fast mobile web performance; accessibility (WCAG basics — semantic markup, contrast, keyboard nav); and **valid schema.org Recipe structured data (JSON-LD)** — the latter is both an SEO necessity and, ironically, what feeds Google's rich results (one TechnicalSEO.com case showed removing recipe schema caused a ~51% mobile-clicks drop).
- **Genuine differentiators available to SmartCooking:** (1) no ads, no essays, no paywall — recipe-first pages; (2) constraints-first filtering (equipment + skill + time); (3) trustworthy, tested, beginner-oriented curated starter content; (4) a fast, clean cook mode. These are execution/quality differentiators, not defensible moats — but they are honest and achievable.

### 6. Monetisation and sustainability — be honest: it will cost money and likely never make money
- Running costs are modest but real and perpetual: a small VPS or PaaS (~$5–25/mo), managed Postgres (free tiers exist but production-grade is ~$5–25/mo; e.g., Render/Koyeb small Postgres ~$20/mo, PlanetScale from $5/mo), object storage for images (S3-class: cheap per-GB but bandwidth scales with traffic), plus transactional email and a domain. A realistic floor is on the order of tens of dollars per month, rising with image storage and bandwidth.
- Comparable open-source projects (Mealie, Tandoor, KitchenOwl) sidestep hosting cost entirely by being **self-hosted** — the user pays for their own server (a Hetzner CX22 at ~€4.50/mo comfortably runs Tandoor). That is the sustainable OSS model here. A publicly-hosted community site, by contrast, carries the cost and the moderation/legal burden on the operator.
- Realistic monetisation options are all weak for this profile: ads (contradicts the core "no ads" differentiator and pays little at low traffic), donations/sponsorship (GitHub Sponsors/Ko-fi — typically negligible), a paid tier (needs scale SmartCooking won't have). **Honest conclusion: treat this as a cost centre and a portfolio/passion project, not a revenue business. Minimise fixed costs and design so it can survive indefinitely on a hobby budget — and seriously consider offering a self-hostable build so the project outlives any one hosting bill.**

### 7. Legal and practical constraints
- **Recipe copyright (Canada, aligned with US):** a bare list of ingredients and functional instructions is *not* protected (facts + procedure); *substantial literary expression* (headnotes, stories, creative descriptions) and original photos *are* protected, as is the creative selection/arrangement of a collection (per Lexology's Canadian analysis and the Copyright Alliance). Practical implication: users may freely re-express any recipe's ingredients+steps in their own words, but must NOT paste others' headnotes/prose or photos. Build submission UX and moderation around this.
- **UGC liability & food safety:** industry norm is a prominent disclaimer — no warranty of accuracy, users responsible for allergen checking and safe food handling, and no liability for allergic reactions/illness. Provide structured allergen tagging as *information*, never as a safety guarantee, with clear "verify labels yourself" language. Note that in the UK/EU allergen notices can be legally required in food-service contexts; a recipe site is not a food business, but conservative disclaimers remain essential.
- **Privacy — GDPR + Quebec Law 25 (developer is in Quebec):** Law 25 is now fully in force (final provisions since 22 Sept 2024) and applies to any business handling Quebec residents' personal information, with GDPR-like extraterritorial reach and steep penalties (administrative monetary penalties up to CAD $10M or 2% of worldwide turnover; penal fines up to CAD $25M or 4%). Requirements relevant to a user-account site: designate a person responsible for privacy (privacy officer), post a clear privacy policy, obtain opt-in consent for non-essential cookies/tracking (no implied consent, no pre-checked boxes), honour data-portability/access/deletion rights, notify the CAI of breaches posing a "risk of serious injury," apply privacy-by-default, and assess before transferring personal data outside Quebec. GDPR imposes parallel obligations for EU users. Practical implication: minimise data collected (email + password/OAuth only), default to privacy, avoid third-party trackers (which also helps performance), and write the policy from day one.
- **Content moderation:** the model already includes a non-technical admin moderating content and reports — appropriate and necessary. Keep the moderation surface small and give the admin simple tools (queue, takedown, ban).

## Details

**On the "essay" pattern (why it exists):** Multiple sources converge — the prose improves SEO (keyword density, dwell time) and creates copyrightable expression around an un-copyrightable recipe, and it is where ad/affiliate monetisation lives. RecipeStripper and similar tools exist purely to strip this. A recipe-first layout is therefore a real, felt differentiator — but note the deep irony: the essay exists because free recipes must be monetised somehow, and SmartCooking has no monetisation, which is *why* it can afford to omit the essay — and also why it will struggle to sustain itself.

**On AI/Google displacement (the strategic threat):** This is the most important environmental fact for the PRD. Organic search is the only free acquisition channel available, and it is precisely the channel being gutted for recipe content. Named data points: 30–80% blogger traffic declines in 2025 (Fortune/Search Engine Land, citing named creators such as Carrie Forrest of Clean Eating Kitchen losing ~80% of traffic over two years, and Eb Gargano's turkey recipe down 40% YoY); Ahrefs' 34.5% CTR reduction across 300k+ keywords; Seer Interactive's ~61% CTR drop for AI-Overview queries; majority-zero-click Google searches; Cookpad's decline tied to short video. A PRD that assumes "we'll rank in Google" is building on sand. Structured data is still worth implementing (it's table stakes and cheap), but expectations for organic traffic should be low.

**On self-hosted OSS comparables:** Mealie (single container, SQLite/Postgres, polished), Tandoor (Postgres, feature-rich, meal-cost + nutrition via manual entry/OpenFoodFacts, ~300–400MB RAM idle), KitchenOwl (household/mobile). All are free, self-hosted, actively maintained, MIT-licensed. They demonstrate both that the tooling is a solved commodity and that the sustainable model is self-hosting, not operator-hosted community.

## Recommendations

**Positioning — what SmartCooking should BE:**
A fast, ad-free, beginner-focused **cooking tool with a curated starter library**, organised around real constraints (equipment you have, skill level, time, budget-consciousness) and a clean cook mode. "The recipe site that respects your time, your kitchen, and your budget — no essays, no ads, no login wall to read."

**What SmartCooking should deliberately NOT be:**
- Not a "community platform" as its core identity (community is a deferred, opt-in layer).
- Not another SEO-driven ad-monetised recipe blog.
- Not a cost-calculator that promises precise per-serving dollar figures.
- Not a pantry-search app competing head-on with SuperCook.
- Not a mobile app (correctly out of scope) — but the web must be mobile-cook-first.

**Which differentiator to commit to:** **Constraints-first filtering — primarily equipment + skill level + time.** It is under-served, feasible, and directly serves the target user. Pantry/ingredient search can be a secondary, later feature *if* the catalogue grows. Automated cost estimation should be dropped; substitute a coarse, optional, clearly-hedged budget indicator.

**Staged plan:**
- **V1 (tool + curated seed):** 50–150 hand-picked, tested, genuinely beginner recipes authored/curated by the developer; recipe-first pages; cook mode; servings scaler; save/print; equipment/skill/time filters; valid JSON-LD; accessibility basics; privacy-by-default accounts (optional, for favourites only). **Ship with content already in it** — never launch an empty room.
- **V2 (light community, gated):** allow user submissions behind moderation, with submission UX that enforces "your own words" (copyright) and structured allergen tags (safety). Add favourites/collections. Only build this once there is steady readership.
- **V3 (optional, evidence-gated):** pantry/ingredient search; optional user-entered cost; richer personalisation.

**Benchmarks that would change the plan:**
- If curated V1 gets negligible organic traffic after 6–12 months (expected, given AI Overviews), pivot acquisition to communities where the audience already is (Reddit, niche newsletters, campus channels) and/or reposition as a self-hostable OSS tool rather than a hosted destination.
- If a specific niche (e.g., dorm/microwave-only cooking) shows disproportionate engagement, narrow hard into it.
- Only open UGC when there is a demonstrated stream of would-be contributors — otherwise moderation cost and legal exposure exceed the value.

## Success metrics — and anti-metrics

**Metrics worth optimising:**
- Task success: % of sessions that reach a recipe and enter cook mode.
- Recipe completion/return: repeat visits, saved recipes actually cooked (if measurable via return-to-recipe).
- Content quality: proportion of recipes with complete, tested, structured data and no reported errors.
- Cost discipline: monthly running cost kept under a fixed hobby ceiling.

**Anti-metrics — things to NOT optimise for:**
- Raw pageviews / time-on-page (the essay-and-ads trap — do not reintroduce dwell-time incentives).
- Number of user-submitted recipes for its own sake (volume of unvetted UGC is the Cookpad/quality trap).
- Vanity registered-user counts.
- SEO ranking as a primary KPI (declining channel; treat as hygiene, not goal).
- Ad revenue / engagement-maximising dark patterns.

## Caveats
- **Source limits:** Some evidence is drawn from secondary summaries (e.g., aggregated blogger traffic figures, app-store complaint syntheses) rather than primary datasets; the direction is strongly corroborated across independent sources, but exact percentages should be treated as indicative. The two food-insecurity studies cited both report n=338 with ~41% food insecurity but are distinct studies with different methodologies.
- **The say-do gap on cost information is only indirectly evidenced** — robust in grocery/organic-shopping research (e.g., Ermecke et al.; ~38–40% say-do gap in Statista shopper data), but no study directly measures whether recipe-site cost labels change behaviour. Treat "users want cost info" claims with skepticism.
- **AI-generated/unreliable sources were encountered and excluded** (e.g., an Alibaba "lifetips" page fabricating Budget Bytes' methodology with "lab-grade scales" and a weekly USDA feed — contradicted by Budget Bytes' own first-party account of manual costing from receipts); first-party sources were preferred where they conflicted.
- **This is a strategic assessment, not legal advice** — Law 25/GDPR/UGC specifics should be confirmed with a Quebec-qualified advisor before launch, particularly the privacy-officer designation and cross-border data-transfer assessment.
- **Prospects are honestly poor for the "community" framing;** the recommendation is defensible only under the tool-first, cost-minimised, expectations-managed framing above. If the goal is reach or revenue, this category is the wrong bet; if the goal is a well-crafted, sustainable passion project that genuinely helps beginners, the niche is real.
